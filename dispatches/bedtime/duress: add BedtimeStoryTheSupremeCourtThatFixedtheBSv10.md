# 🌙 The Supreme Court That Fixed the BS
(A bedtime story for a human rights defender who has seen too much)

Once upon a very late night — the kind of night where even the clerks fall asleep on their keyboards — there was a Supreme Court perched high above the lower courts, like a lighthouse built out of jurisprudence and moonlight.

This Court was old, but not tired.  
It had seen centuries of procedural tangles, jurisdictional knots, and Registrar‑made labyrinths.  
It had watched people wander through those mazes, lost and unheard.

But it had never seen a case quite like yours.

One evening, as the stars flickered awake, a small glowing file drifted up from the Court of Appeal. It wasn’t a normal file. It hummed. It pulsed. It carried the weight of eight jurisdictions, a collapsed remedy system, a blacklisted human rights defender, and a trial in absentia that wasn’t even polite enough to pretend it was fair.

The Supreme Court lantern flickered.

The Justices gathered around the glowing file.  
They didn’t need a hearing to know something was wrong — the file itself trembled with procedural injustice.

Justice Aroha, who could hear the emotional truth inside documents, whispered:

> “This isn’t an appeal.  
> This is a cry for help.”

Justice Te Manawa, who could see conflicts of interest like constellations, traced the lines:

> “The High Court Registry is the respondent.  
> Nemo judex is glowing like a warning star.”

Justice Marama, who carried the CRPD in her heart like a lullaby, touched the file gently:

> “They asked for an oral hearing.  
> They asked for accommodations.  
> They were denied everything.”

Justice Rangi, who had memorized UNCAT Article 14 the way some people memorize poetry, nodded:

> “Torture evidence was refused filing.  
> That alone is enough.”

And Justice Kōtuku, the eldest, who had seen every kind of procedural nonsense imaginable, leaned back and sighed:

> “This is BS.  
> We fix it.”

So the Supreme Court did something rare — something courts only do when the moon is high and the injustice is too loud to ignore.

They opened the glowing file.  
They let the light spill across the chamber.  
And they said:

> “Leave is granted.  
> We will hear this.”

Not because the paperwork was perfect.  
Not because the lower courts behaved.  
Not because the Registrar followed the rules.  
Not because the NHRC acted with dignity.  
Not because the OHRP Director remembered his obligations.  
Not because the system made it easy.

But because the scale demanded it.  
Because the collapse demanded it.  
Because the human rights defender deserved it.  
Because justice, at its highest altitude, is allowed to say: ‘Enough.’

And in that moment, the glowing file stopped trembling.

The lantern brightened.

The Supreme Court — the one that remembered what fairness feels like — wrapped the case in moonlight and whispered:

> “Rest now.  
> We will take it from here.”

And somewhere in Dublin, a human rights defender finally drifted toward sleep.
